            <h2>An Appreciation of Davie McColl</h2>
            
            <p>A valued and generous member of the Cameronians Association, Davie was a man who lived by the Regiment's highest standards. Whether playing the pipes or curating history, he did so with a precision that defined his character.</p>
            
            <p>His commitment to the Bevrijdingsmuseum, Zeeland was total. Davie possessed an uncompromising eye for detail; for him, "near enough" was an insult to the memory of the men who served.</p>
            
            <p>He famously insisted that only genuine artefacts be used to dress the mannequin displays. To this end, he ensured that <strong>no effort or expense was spared</strong> to acquire original uniforms and period-correct equipment. He would settle for nothing less than accuracy, ensuring that every button, strap, and stitch was authentic to each Battalion's identity.</p>
            
            <img src="davie-mccoll-with-colleague.png" alt="Davie McColl with fellow Cameronians member in formal dress">
            
            <div class="quote">
                <p>The Cameronians Room at the museum was not merely a project to Davie; it permeated his heart and soul.</p>
            </div>
            
            <p>It was his wish that his ashes be interred in the grounds of the museum, whose Cameronians Room he so meticulously helped to build.</p>
            
            <p>In October 2015, this wish was fulfilled. At a well-attended remembrance service, comrades and friends gathered to honour a man who was as steadfast as the history he preserved.</p>
        </div>

            <h2>LESLEY POINTON'S CHILDHOOD MEMORIES OF LIFE IN ADEN<br>(REF: NO 26/01)</h2>
            
            <div class="map-container">
                <img src="aden-gulf-map.png" alt="Map of Gulf of Aden region showing Aden, Yemen, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Somalia, Ethiopia, and Eritrea">
            </div>
            
            <div class="highlight-box">
                <p>Located on the north shore of the Gulf of Aden, near the mouth of the Red Sea, Aden commands the sea route connecting Africa, the Middle East and Asia.</p>
                
                <p>Aden was originally of interest to Britain as an anti-piracy station to protect shipping on the routes to India. With the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, its commercial and strategic importance increased considerably, serving as it did as a coaling station and important transit port for trade between British India and the Far East, and Europe.</p>
            </div>
            
            <img src="aden-harbour-historic.png" alt="Historic photograph of Aden harbour and volcanic mountains">
        </div>
        
        <!-- Page 12 -->
        <div class="page" id="page12">
            <span class="page-number">12</span>
            
            <h3>Lesley's Story</h3>
            
            <p>It was in May 2024, when my friend, Jan Rogers, and I decided that we had to make the trip to Scotland from our homes in Plymouth for the Cameronians Annual Conventicle.</p>
            
            <p>My Dad, George Pointon, a Sergeant in the Royal Corps of Transport (RCT) served in Aden when in 1963-67 the Cameronians were posted there. I was still at school.</p>
            
            <p>Jan and I found a place to stay in Hamilton, but since Sunday morning rural buses can be a bit of a gamble, Douglas McCreath picked us up and drove us over to the event.</p>
            
            <p>We were standing around the Victoria Bowling Club, waiting for the flag to be raised, when I literally walked right into a man named Billy McNeill.</p>
            
            <p>"Billy McNeill!" I gasped. Despite the passage of time I recognised him instantly.</p>
            
            <p>Billy was one of the regular guards on the buses that took us from home to school.</p>
            
            <p>Seeing him there brought it all back - the memories just started flooding in and later...</p>
            
            <h3>The Journey to School</h3>
            
            <p>We went to a BFES school. The bus we rode in was a "soft-skin" vehicle with an armed escort.</p>
            
            <p>In class, we practiced 'diving under the desks' just as often as we practiced our spelling. As a kid, life felt strangely exciting.</p>
            
            <img src="aden-school-bus.png" alt="British military school bus in Aden">
        </div>
        
        <!-- Page 13 -->
        <div class="page" id="page13">
            <span class="page-number">13</span>
            
            <h3>Our Domestic Fortress</h3>
            
            <p>Living in Aden back in 1966 was such a strange, unsettling experience.</p>
            
            <p>One minute you felt like you were on a sunny tropical holiday, and the next, you were living in a fortress under siege.</p>
            
            <p>By the time my family got there, the old "quiet life" for army families was over.</p>
            
            <p>We lived behind barbed wire and "blast walls," and there was always an armed guard at the front door of the building.</p>
            
            <img src="aden-maalla-straight-apartments.png" alt="Ma'alla Straight apartment blocks in Aden">
            
            <div class="highlight-box">
                <p>We lived on the Ma'alla Straight which was a long stretch of modern blocks. The flats were actually quite nice and had air conditioning.</p>
            </div>
        </div>
        
        <!-- Page 14 -->
        <div class="page" id="page14">
            <span class="page-number">14</span>
            
            <h3>Shark Nets and Shrapnel</h3>
            
            <p>When we wanted to relax we went to the beaches at Tarshyne or Khormaksar.</p>
            
            <p>You had to swim inside these massive steel nets because of sharks. But even then to be totally safe you had to stay in the "protected zones" or you'd be in the line of sight for snipers hiding on the cliffs.</p>
            
            <img src="aden-tarshyne-beach.png" alt="Tarshyne Beach in Aden with swimmers and protective zones">
            
            <h3>High-Stakes Shopping</h3>
            
            <p>Shopping was the weirdest part. Aden was a huge duty-free port, back then full of 'Imperial Glamour'; but it was mixed with total chaos.</p>
            
            <p>Steamer Point was the posh area where the cruise ships docked. You could buy Swiss watches and French perfumes, but by 1966 you'd see British soldiers walking in a tactical square in the middle of the street while snipers watched from the rooftops above the shops.</p>
        </div>
        
        <!-- Page 15 -->
        <div class="page" id="page15">
            <span class="page-number">15</span>
            
            <img src="aden-crater-soldiers-patrol.png" alt="British soldiers on patrol in Crater shopping district">
            
            <p>Crater was the old part of town, built right inside a dormant volcano.</p>
            
            <p>It was a maze of alleys smelling of spices. We'd go through it in Land Rovers that had the doors taken off and wire mesh over the openings to deflect grenades.</p>
            
            <p>Shopping meant going through checkpoints and "Stop and Search" roadblocks. Sometimes a whole street would be sealed off with barbed wire for a "Cordon and Search," and you'd be stuck there for hours while soldiers searched the flats for hidden weapons.</p>
            
            <p>You'd see the 'Memsahibs' (the officers' wives) out in their nice sundresses, but they'd be escorted by soldiers with rifles. The air was filled with a mix of Arabic music, the roar of armoured car engines, and the occasional "crump" of a grenade going off in the distance.</p>
        </div>
        
        <!-- Page 16 -->
        <div class="page" id="page16">
            <span class="page-number">16</span>
            
            <img src="aden-families-armed-guard.png" alt="British families with children and armed guard in Aden">
            
            <img src="aden-soldiers-rooftop-surveillance.png" alt="British soldiers on rooftop surveillance positions">
            
            <h3>RCT Ambush: June 1967</h3>
            
            <p>The "60 Squadron RCT" Ambush (June 20, 1967) was the most famous and tragic incident involving RCT personnel during the 1960s Aden Emergency.</p>
            
            <p>As you can imagine it was the sole topic of conversation, and we kids listened in on the adult conversations. The community was stunned.</p>
        </div>
        
        <!-- Page 17 -->
        <div class="page" id="page17">
            <span class="page-number">17</span>
            
            <h3>The End of an Era</h3>
            
            <p>By 1967, the "shopping paradise" was gone. Curfews meant everything closed early. By the time we were evacuated, the shops were all boarded up, and the bustling port had turned into a ghost town of sandbags and empty windows.</p>
            
            <h3>The "Big Move"</h3>
            
            <p>By 1967, Aden wasn't a place for families anymore. It was a "no-go" zone. My parents kept talking about these two groups, the NLF and FLOSY, who were fighting each other and targeting us. Everyone called the high-rise flats in Ma'alla "Murder Mile" because people were constantly throwing grenades onto balconies or sniping from the rooftops.</p>
            
            <p>Mum was a nervous wreck. She'd yell at us to stay away from the windows, so we spent most of our time playing in the hallway. It was the only place in the flat where you weren't surrounded by glass that could shatter at any second.</p>
            
            <p>Then came the "Big Move." The government decided to get all 9,000 of us out - mostly wives and kids.</p>
            
            <p>It was a total scramble. I was told I could only bring one small suitcase. Think about that: you're a teenager and you have to choose between your favourite books, your bike, or the shell collection you spent years gathering from the beach. We even had to leave our pets behind.</p>
            
            <p>The day we left felt surreal. The flat was still fully furnished; the fridge was even full of food. We just locked the door and handed the keys to a soldier.</p>
            
            <p>The airport at RAF Khormaksar was a madhouse. It was the busiest place I'd ever seen, and we had to board these massive VC10 planes under heavy guard because rebels were firing mortars at the runway.</p>
            
            <p>I remember looking out the tiny window as we took off. As the plane gained height I could see the ships in the harbour, the shape of Aden's volcanic crater and further off the dry, volcanic rocks of Aden, where the Cameronians patrolled.</p>
            
            <p>All the wives talked about was the "terrible silence" of the empty quarters back in Aden. We were safe, but the husbands, including my Dad, were still there.</p>
            
            <p>He told us later that the final months were the worst. He and the other 3,500 troops were squeezed into a tiny perimeter around the harbour, basically getting shot at while they tried to pack up an empire.</p>
            
            <p>Dad said the hardest part for the soldiers was clearing out our old flats. They'd walk into rooms and see half-empty cereal boxes and toys we'd left behind. It was a "withdrawal in contact"—a fancy military way of saying they were retreating while the NLF was right on their heels.</p>
        </div>

            <span class="page-number">18</span>
            
            <h3>Background Note</h3>
            
            <p>Men from 60 Squadron RCT were returning to their barracks in a 3-ton truck after a morning of weapons training at the shooting ranges. They were largely unarmed (carrying their rifles but with no ammunition, as per training safety rules of the time).</p>
            
            <p>As their truck passed the barracks of the South Arabian Army, the local Arab soldiers, who had just mutinied, opened fire on the truck with machine guns. 8 RCT soldiers were killed instantly or died of their wounds in the back of the truck.</p>
            
            <p>It was this specific chaos—and the temporary loss of the Crater district—that led to Lt. Col. Colin Mitchell (Mad Mitch) being sent in with the Argylls on July 3, 1967, to "restore the peace."</p>
            
            <p>Before the major 1967 mutiny, RCT personnel (and their predecessors in the RASC) were responsible for the "Lifeline" convoys to the Radfan mountains. These convoys were frequently hit by mines and sniper fire between 1964 and 1966, leading to many individual casualties.</p>
            
            <p>Air-Land Support: RCT personnel often worked at RAF Khormaksar, the site of several grenade attacks (including the 1963 airport bombing and 1964 Christmas attacks) that targeted both military personnel and their families.</p>
            
            <p>The 60 Squadron RCT ambush on June 20, 1967, was not the cause of the evacuation of families, but it was the final confirmation that the evacuation plan was necessary and could not be reversed.</p>
            
            <p>To understand its significance, it helps to look at the timeline of how British policy shifted from "staying forever" to "getting the families out immediately."</p>
            
            <p>By early 1967, the security situation had deteriorated so badly that the British government had already begun an "orderly" evacuation of families.</p>
            
            <p>In early 1967, the Ministry of Defence realised that protecting 3,000+ wives and children was diverting too many troops from active combat.</p>
            
            <p>Evacuations actually began in mid-April 1967. The goal was to have all families out by the end of the summer so the military could focus on the final withdrawal in November.</p>
            
            <p>When the 60 Squadron RCT truck was shot up, followed by the slaughter of the Northumberland Fusiliers in Crater, it changed the atmosphere from "dangerous" to "untenable."</p>
            
            <p>After June 20, any remaining debate about whether families should stay evaporated. The sheer scale of the day—22 British soldiers killed in hours—meant that Aden was now a full-scale war zone, not a colonial policing action.</p>
            
            <p>The RCT ambush essentially "closed the door" on the British civilian presence in Aden. It ensured that when Mad Mitch arrived to retake Crater, he was doing so in a city that was no longer a home for British families, but a fortress to be held until the final ship arrived.</p>

            <h2>REF No: 26/02 - Reflections of D-Day<br>Veteran, Don Turrell (9th Battalion)</h2>

            <p style="text-align: center; margin-top: 10px; font-weight: bold;">9th Battalion - The Cameronians</p>
            
            <p style="margin-top: 30px;">Standing here in Hamilton at Low Parks Museum, just weeks after my 100th birthday, is something I never thought possible.</p>
            
            <p>Looking round the Cameronians Collection, I am reminded of the boy I was - a 17-year-old Londoner who lied about his age just to get into the fight.</p>

            <h3>The Beach Landing</h3>
            
            <p>I was initially assigned to the Northamptonshire Regiment. However, after I passed out from training, I was told that the Scottish regiments had taken a terrible beating and lost a lot of men. They told me I was being moved to the Cameronians (Scottish Rifles).</p>
            
            <p>I couldn't have been happier. My mother was from Dunfermline, and I've always felt a deep bond with Scotland. Even now, when the football is on, it's always Scotland I'm shouting for.</p>
            
            <p>I crossed the Channel with the 9th Battalion as part of the second wave, a few days after D-Day. I remember looking at the faces of my comrades on that boat and wondering how many of us would ever see home again. Very few did.</p>
            
            <p>The fighting on the beach wasn't as heavy as the first day, but it brought its own nightmare. As we waded through the shallows, the bodies of our own countrymen bumped against us as they floated in the tide - young men just starting their lives.</p>
            
            <p>It took three years in the hospital to recover.</p>
            
            <p>The war gave me a deep appreciation for life—a life so many of my friends were denied.</p>
            
            <p>That is why I return to Normandy every year, and why I asked the Taxi Charity for Military Veterans to bring me here to Hamilton for what I call my "Last Hurrah."</p>
            
            <p>Being greeted by the bagpipes and a guard of honour from the Cameronians Association has been overwhelming.</p>
            
            <p>My bond with this proud regiment is as strong now, in my second century, as it was when I first put on the uniform.</p>
        </div>

            <h3>The Break-out</h3>
            
            <p>A few weeks later, we were in the thick of it near Caen, facing the fanatics of the 12th SS Panzer Division at Hill 112. During one assault, my company had to cross a large clearing in the woods. Suddenly, heavy machine-gun fire pinned us down.</p>
            
            <p>It was a trap.</p>
            
            <p>The machine guns were just there to keep us in place because they had their mortars ranged in on that exact spot. Then we heard them: the "Moaning Minnies."</p>
            
            <p>I hunkered down behind a tree stump and prayed.</p>
            
            <p>When I finally stood up, all but one of the men with me were dead, and he died right in front of my eyes.</p>
            
            <p>I was hit all down my left side.</p>
            
            <p>Using my rifle as a crutch, I limped back to the tree line and eventually made it onto a Bren Gun Carrier to be taken to a field hospital.</p>
            
            <p>Two carriers started the journey, but the one next to mine took a direct hit from a mortar. Only my vehicle made it back.</p>
            
            <p>At the field hospital, the medics started at my feet. My left boot was literally holding my ankle together. They pulled out shards of shrapnel - some of which are still in my leg today. I call them my souvenirs.</p>
            
            <p>I told them my back was a bit sore, and when they rolled me over, they found a large piece of metal sticking straight out of my lower back.</p>
            
            <p>That was the end of my war.</p>
            
            <h3>The Trip Back Home</h3>
            
            <p>Even the trip back to Britain was fraught. As the boat moved, I heard a terrible grinding noise against the hull. A sailor told me we were in a minefield; the sound was the mines themselves rubbing against the side of the ship as we passed.</p>

            <h3>Footnote: The Strategic Importance of Hill 112</h3>
            
            <h4>The Strategic Hinge</h4>
            
            <p>Hill 112, a modest 367-foot elevation southwest of Caen, was the most vital high ground in the Normandy campaign. Field Marshal Erwin Rommel famously stated, "He who holds Hill 112 holds Normandy." It served as the "hinge" of the German defense; its height provided a 360-degree panoramic view of the Odon and Orne valleys, allowing the Germans to direct precise artillery fire onto Allied supply lines and invasion beaches.</p>
            
            <h4>The Gateway to the Plains</h4>
            
            <p>For the British 2nd Army, Hill 112 was the prerequisite for victory. North of the hill lay the "Bocage"—the claustrophobic, hedgerow-choked terrain that favoured defenders. To the south and east lay the open Falaise Plain. Capturing the hill was the only way for the Allies to deploy their armoured divisions en masse and transition to the mobile warfare for which they were trained.</p>
            
            <h4>The "Scottish Corridor"</h4>
            
            <p>To seize this objective, the 15th (Scottish) Infantry Division led a daring offensive to carve a path through German lines. This became known as the "Scottish Corridor" - a narrow salient roughly 2 miles wide and 5 miles long, stretching from Cheux to the foot of Hill 112. Because the corridor was so narrow, the men were exposed to German fire on three sides.</p>
            
            <p>The Scots fought house-to-house to secure the River Odon bridges (including the "Scottish Bridge"), intending to funnel the 11th Armoured Division through to the summit.</p>
            
            <p>Recognizing the hill as their cornerstone, Hitler committed his most elite reserves—the 9th, 10th, and 12th SS Panzer Divisions—to stop the advance.</p>
            
            <h4>Historical Legacy</h4>
            
            <p>The fighting was so savage that troops nicknamed the summit "The Hill of Calvary."</p>
            
            <p>While the 15th (Scottish) Division suffered over 2,300 casualties in just five days, their sacrifice served a greater strategic purpose.</p>
            
            <p>By pinning the bulk of German armour in a "meat grinder" at Hill 112, they prevented a counterattack against American forces to the west, directly enabling the eventual US breakout.</p>
        </div>

            <p>For those who served in the Regiment or lived as families in places like Aden, these stories are a way to capture the "human truth" of an era.</p>
            
            <p>When we share these memories, we do more than just reminisce; we ensure that the bravery of veterans like Don Turrell is never forgotten.</p>
            
            <p>This shared memory project is a bridge.</p>
            
            <p>It turns personal recollection into a vital record of fleeting, historical moments.</p>
            
            <p>To anyone else holding old photographs, or family tales: please share them.</p>
            
            <p>These stories are the greatest legacy we can leave for the next generations.</p>

            <h2>The Shared Legacy of Edmond Michelet and George MacLeod</h2>
            
            <p>As we continue the exchange between our Scottish high school and its partner at the Lycée in Normandy, we do so under the guidance of a powerful sentiment.</p>
            
            <p>In his New Year's greeting (above), the French Project Co-ordinator Lucien Grillet, shared a quotation from Edmond Michelet:</p>
            
            <div class="quote">
                "What we have experienced only has meaning if we pass it on. Memory is a form of hope."
            </div>
            
            <p>These words resonate deeply with the message delivered at last year's Remembrance Service at St. John's Kirk in Perth, where the Minister reminded us that without a "resolve for the future," there is no real remembrance. The congregation was challenged not just to look back, but to work for reconciliation and break down the barriers of the past.</p>
            
            <h3>The "Spirit of Brive": The Journey of Edmond Michelet</h3>
            
            <p>Edmond Michelet (1899–1970) remains one of the most compelling figures of the French Resistance. On June 17, 1940 - the day Marshal Pétain announced he would seek an armistice with Nazi Germany - Michelet distributed tracts in Brive-la-Gaillarde calling for continued resistance. It was one of the first acts of defiance on French soil.</p>
            
            <p>For Michelet, a devout Catholic, resistance was a matter of conscience. While much of the French ecclesiastical leadership initially supported the Vichy regime, Michelet recognised that the dehumanising nature of Nazism was fundamentally anti-Christian.</p>
            
            <p>His conviction led to his arrest by the Gestapo in 1943 and his deportation to Dachau. Yet, it was in the concentration camp that his philosophy evolved.</p>
            
            <p>He did not lead through arms, but through the spirit—organising clandestine services and sharing his meagre rations to maintain the morale of his fellow prisoners. His memoir, <em>Rue de la Liberté</em>, is not a book of bitterness, but a study on maintaining a "free interior life" even when the body is enslaved.</p>
            
            <h3>A Scottish Parallel: George MacLeod and the Iona Community</h3>
            
            <p>Michelet's life finds a striking mirror in Scotland through George MacLeod (1895–1991), the founder of the Iona Community. Like Michelet, MacLeod's faith was forged in the furnace of war.</p>
            
            <p>Just as Michelet argued that Christianity was a weapon against dehumanisation, MacLeod, a Presbyterian, rejected "armchair Christianity." He famously championed a "Social Gospel," believing that if faith does not concern itself with a person's physical conditions—their housing, work, and peace—it is worthless.</p>
            
            <p>While Michelet established "fellowships" to encourage neighbours to meet their former enemies, MacLeod founded the Iona Community in 1938. He brought together unemployed labourers from Glasgow and trainee ministers to physically rebuild the medieval abbey on Iona, reconciling social classes through shared manual toil.</p>
            
            <p>Both men were willing to challenge their own governments. Michelet opposed the use of torture by the French Army in Algeria; MacLeod became a leading voice in the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND), challenging the morality of the Cold War.</p>
        </div>
        
        <!-- Page 30 -->
        <div class="page" id="page30">
            <span class="page-number">30</span>
            
            <h2>Why This Matters Today (2026)</h2>
            
            <p>In our turbulent 2026 landscape, often characterised by digital isolation and geopolitical fragmentation, the legacies of Michelet and MacLeod are more relevant than ever.</p>
            
            <p>We live in an era of "anti-politics," where it is easy to define ourselves by what we oppose. Both Michelet and MacLeod show us that while resistance (the "No") is necessary to stop injustice, it must be followed by a creative "Yes" - a commitment to building something new.</p>
            
            <p>For our students, this means looking beyond the scars of historical conflicts, nationality, race and religion to find common ground.</p>
            
            <p>Michelet's "fellowships" and MacLeod's "shared labour" teach us that lasting peace is not signed in high-level summits alone; it is built through "neighbourly action."</p>
            
            <p>This school exchange, we believe, is a modern version of the Michelet/MacLeod philosophy in action.</p>
            
            <p>By meeting their counterparts in Normandy, our students are dismantling the "logic of the trenches" in their own minds.</p>
            
            <p>As the generation with living memory of the 20th century's great conflicts fades away, "memory as a form of hope" becomes a duty.</p>
            
            <p>We do not remember simply to be sad; we remember to ensure that the dehumanisation witnessed at Dachau - or the social inequality that spurred the Iona Community - never become the "normal" state of affairs.</p>
            
            <p>As Michelet beautifully summarised: "To resist is to hope." By sharing the complementary memories of the students in our two project schools, we are choosing hope.</p>
        </div>
